# Research Proposal

## Working title
**"Is invasional meltdown real? A global, retrospectively-validated test of facilitation among freshwater invaders"**

(Working alternative: *"Predicting the next freshwater invasional meltdown: a validated global early-warning test"*)

(Prior working title, kept for reference: *"Global compounding risk from co-occurring freshwater invasive species: a predictive test of invasional meltdown"* — see Section 3a, 2026-09-05, for why the framing shifted from a risk map to a hypothesis test.)

---

## 1. Background and motivation

- Invasional meltdown — the hypothesis that non-native species facilitate one another's establishment and impact (Simberloff & Von Holle, 1999) — is a 25+ year old, heavily cited concept (>3,100 citations on the founding paper).
- Despite this, the evidence base is almost entirely built from **single-site case studies** (Christmas Island crazy ants, Great Lakes, San Francisco Bay, individual plant-soil systems) or **theoretical/review papers**. There is no global, predictive, spatially-explicit test of the hypothesis.
- The most current authoritative synthesis (**Ricciardi & Simberloff, 2025**, *Biological Invasions*) explicitly states that projections of future invasion dynamics are limited by "*limits in understanding of causal relationships between species occurrences, environmental changes, drivers of biological invasions, and the roles of dispersal, **biotic interactions**, and impacts caused by invasive alien species*" — naming multi-species interaction effects as an open, unaddressed gap in current modeling.
- **Braga et al. (2018)** systematically reviewed 150 invasional-meltdown studies and confirmed "broad support but with biases and gaps" — reinforcing that the theory is under-tested outside case-study contexts.
- **Seebens et al. (2025)**, a large IPBES-linked consortium assessment in *Biological Reviews*, found that of all realms, **aquatic/freshwater systems carry among the largest documented knowledge gaps** in global alien-species status and trend assessment, and explicitly named "biotic interactions" and spatial-resolution as unresolved challenges (their six key challenges, Fig. 8).
- Meanwhile, **species distribution modeling (SDM)** of invasive species is mature and well-established (used extensively for single-species and even multi-species inventories, e.g. Early et al. 2016, *Nature Communications*, ~900 species), but no study has used it to explicitly map **where multiple independently-introduced invaders' predicted ranges overlap**, as a quantitative signal of compounding/meltdown risk — as opposed to mapping single-species risk or cataloguing established distributions.

**The gap, stated plainly:** nobody has combined (a) global SDM/occurrence-based range prediction, (b) multiple co-occurring freshwater invaders across taxa, and (c) explicit cross-referencing against real-world co-occurrence records and biodiversity value, to predict where the *next* invasional meltdown is most likely — before it shows up in the observational record.

### A directly complementary hook
Wang et al. (2026, *Nature Climate Change*) show that climate warming is fragmenting native freshwater fish habitat via "thermal barriers," forcing native fish into shrinking cool-water refugia. Using the same underlying climate/streamflow data (FutureStreams), this project can test whether those same shrinking refugia are simultaneously becoming more climatically suitable for multiple invasive species — a "double jeopardy" narrative that directly complements, rather than competes with, their finding.

### The extinction-crisis anchor
**Overed-Sayer et al. (2025, *Nature*)** — a ~90-author IUCN consortium assessment of 23,496 freshwater decapods, fishes and odonates — found that **one-quarter of assessed freshwater fauna is threatened with extinction**, and named invasive species as one of the prevalent threats alongside pollution, dams and overharvesting. This is the calibration point for what "matters enough" looks like to a flagship journal in this exact subfield, and it gives this project a direct, citable hook: if compounding invasion-risk zones can be shown to concentrate disproportionately in the same geography as that assessment's threatened-species hotspots, the paper's central claim moves from "here is a risk map" to "here is a mechanism contributing to a documented, Nature-published extinction crisis." See Section 3a for how this reframes the research questions and Section 6 for the added methods step.

## 2. Research questions

**Headline question (2026-09-05 reframe — see Section 3a):** Is invasional meltdown — facilitation between independently-introduced invaders — a real, statistically detectable, globally generalizable phenomenon in freshwater systems, or does it fail to hold up once tested systematically beyond single-site case studies? The five questions below are the operationalization of that test, not a separate risk-mapping exercise.

1. What is the current (and projected future) climatically/hydrologically suitable range of each shortlisted invasive freshwater species?
2. Where do **multiple** species' predicted suitable ranges spatially co-occur, more than expected under a null/random model — and, aggregated globally, does observed co-occurrence significantly exceed the null expectation overall (the formal test of the meltdown hypothesis itself, not just a description of where it's concentrated)?
3. **(Retrospective validation — the load-bearing addition, see Section 3a and 6 step 5a).** If the model is fit only on occurrence data up to an earlier cutoff (e.g. pre-2015/2018), do the "primed but not yet hit" zones it identifies from that cutoff show significantly elevated real-world multi-species establishment in the years since, compared to zones not flagged as primed? This converts the project from a descriptive snapshot into a demonstrated, verified predictive/early-warning result.
4. Do high-compounding-risk / primed zones disproportionately overlap freshwater biodiversity hotspots and high-endemism areas — and, more specifically, do they overlap the threatened-species geography identified by Overed-Sayer et al. (2025, *Nature*)'s IUCN freshwater fauna assessment, i.e. is compounding invasion risk concentrated where documented extinction risk is already highest?
5. (Extension) Do projected future refugia for native fish (per Wang et al. 2026-style thermal-barrier logic) coincide with zones of *increasing* invader-overlap suitability under the same climate forcing — a "double jeopardy" test?

## 3. Novelty statement (for the introduction)

Building on Gallardo and colleagues' decade of work mapping multi-species invasion risk in Great Britain and Europe — including a global, cross-taxa human-footprint model (Gallardo et al. 2015) that stacked 72 species into a cumulative world heat map — we provide the first test of the invasional meltdown hypothesis that is simultaneously global in scope, freshwater-specific (using river-network-aware environmental data rather than coarse global climate layers), explicitly framed around facilitation rather than human footprint, tested against a null/randomized expectation, validated against real documented co-occurrence, and cross-referenced against freshwater biodiversity value.

### Systematic review pass 3 (2026-09-02) — Mahapatra et al. (2026) full-text read

Full text (not just abstract) confirms this is the closest methodological
precedent found in the review so far. Method: individual MaxEnt SDMs for 64
invasive mammal species (single algorithm, not an ensemble), thresholded to
binary suitable/unsuitable at the 10th-percentile cutoff, then **simply
summed into a 4-category stacked map** — "No. of invasive mammals" per grid
cell (0 / 1–10 / 11–20 / 21–30+) — for the current climate and two future
SSP scenarios (Fig. 2). This is mechanically the same "count species per
cell" step as this project's proposed compounding-risk metric (Section 6,
step 4), done at global scale, already published.

**Confirmed absent from Mahapatra et al. — this is now the explicit basis
for the novelty claim, not scope alone:**
1. No facilitation/invasional-meltdown framing anywhere in the paper — it
   is framed entirely around niche conservatism (the COUE scheme: Stability,
   Unfilling, Expansion) and "hotspots of invasion risk."
2. No null-model or randomization test of whether the observed stacking
   exceeds a random/chance expectation — purely descriptive counting, not a
   statistical test of compounding risk.
3. No validation against real documented multi-species co-occurrence
   records — pure prediction, with no "primed but not yet hit" distinction
   between already-invaded and emerging risk zones.
4. No freshwater biodiversity/endemism overlay.
5. Terrestrial mammals only, not freshwater; single-algorithm MaxEnt, not
   an ensemble; no river-network-aware predictors.

**Action for the introduction/novelty section:** cite Mahapatra et al.
(2026) directly as the closest existing precedent for the stacking
mechanic, and state plainly that this project adds the statistical test,
the real-co-occurrence validation, the biodiversity overlay, and the
freshwater/facilitation framing that it lacks. Do not claim the stacking
method itself as novel.

The user supplied several Gallardo PDFs directly (`references/references_pdfs/`).
Full-text reads surfaced the closest precedent in the entire review:

**Gallardo, Zieritz & Aldridge (2015, *PLoS ONE*)** — "The Importance of

**What still differentiates this project from it, precisely:**
1. Their heat map mixes all 72 species across all 4 taxon-habitat groups

2. Coarse global 9×9km climate/geology/human-footprint predictors, no
   river-network-aware hydrological data (no HydroATLAS/Hydrography90m

3. Framed entirely around the human footprint as a predictor variable, not
   facilitation/meltdown between invaders.
4. No null-model significance test that the stacking pattern exceeds a

5. No validation against real documented multi-species co-occurrence (no
   "primed but not yet hit" distinction).
6. No biodiversity/endemism overlay.
7. Single-algorithm MaxEnt, not ensemble.
8. Species list chosen for NW-Europe (GB/France/Belgium/Netherlands)

**A fourth Gallardo & Aldridge paper** was also found and read in full:
**Gallardo & Aldridge (2013, *Biological Conservation*)**, "Evaluating the
combined threat of climate change and biological invasions on endangered
species." Uses BIOMOD ensemble SDMs (the exact tool named in Section 8) for

internally numbered 2013a/b/c in their own later citations.

**The central realization of this whole review:** nearly every serious
precedent found across all six search passes — the three-to-four 2013
papers, the 2015 meltdown paper, this 2015 human-footprint paper, the 2018

This project's introduction should not frame itself against "the field" in

## 6. Methods outline

1. **Occurrence data acquisition & cleaning**: pull GBIF + SInAS records per species; filter to freshwater/inland only (remove marine/estuarine misassignments — a known contamination issue for fish records); deduplicate; spatially thin to reduce sampling-bias inflation.
2. **Predictor assembly**: extract HydroATLAS static variables + FutureStreams derived bioclim-style variables at occurrence points, snapped to the river network (not naive lat/long extraction).
3. **Individual species SDMs**: ensemble modeling (e.g. `biomod2`) per species — multiple algorithms, cross-validated, current + future (per RCP/warming-level) projections.
4. **Compounding-risk metric**: overlay species' predicted suitable-range rasters; define an overlap/stacking index (e.g. count of species predicted suitable per cell, or a weighted index); test whether observed overlap exceeds a null/randomized expectation (spatial randomization test). **This is the paper's central hypothesis test as of the 2026-09-05 reframe (Section 3a), not a supporting check** — report a global effect size and significance for whether observed multi-species overlap exceeds the null, framed as the first systematic test of whether invasional meltdown is real and detectable at scale. Ground this step explicitly in the stacked-SDM/SESAM literature (D'Amen et al. 2015/2019; the 2023 *Integrative Zoology* stacked-model error-propagation paper — Section 12 pass 9) rather than treating the stacking mechanic as ad hoc.
5. **Validation against reality**: cross-reference high-overlap cells against actual documented multi-species co-occurrence (from GBIF/SInAS/CABI records) to separate "already-invaded" from "primed but not yet hit" — the latter is the genuinely predictive, actionable output.
5a. **(New, 2026-09-05 — the load-bearing addition for a higher-tier claim, Section 3a).** Repeat steps 3-5 using only occurrence data up to a historical cutoff (candidate 2015 or 2018 — confirm feasibility first, see Section 11), then test whether the "primed but not yet hit" zones identified *from that cutoff* show significantly elevated real multi-species establishment in the post-cutoff record versus zones not flagged as primed. This is a retrospective demonstration of predictive skill, not just a current-day map — the single addition most likely to move this from a strong disciplinary paper to a Nature Communications-tier claim. Gate: requires sufficient occurrence density both pre- and post-cutoff per species; check before committing (Section 11).
6. **Biodiversity-value cross-reference**: overlay "primed" zones against WWF freshwater ecoregions/endemism data, **and explicitly against the threatened-species geography from Overed-Sayer et al. (2025, *Nature*)'s IUCN freshwater fauna assessment** (Section 1, "extinction-crisis anchor"), to flag where ecological stakes are highest and to tie the result directly to a documented, flagship-published extinction crisis.
7. **(Extension) Native-refugia overlay**: compare against Wang et al. (2026)-style thermal-refugia logic using the same FutureStreams data, to test the "double jeopardy" hypothesis (Q5). Candidate method: Lee et al. (2025)'s Refugia Habitat Metric (RHM — down-weights native suitability by invader suitability, negative-exponential, tunable per-invader weighting) is a strong fit and directly reuses brown bullhead catfish (already in the shortlist, Section 4); worth discussing with Kusabs (co-author, my PhD supervisor) before building a metric from scratch.

## 3a. Scope reframe for high-end journal fit (2026-09-05)

**Decision, discussed with the user 2026-09-05:** the project's underlying data, species list, and methods pipeline (Sections 4-8) are unchanged. What changes is the framing and one added methods step, moving the target from "a strong GCB/Diversity and Distributions paper" to "a credible shot at Nature Communications or above."

**Why the prior framing capped out below Nature-tier:** Sections 1-2 (pre-reframe) were written as a *descriptive risk-mapping exercise* — "here's where compounding risk is, here's who's primed." That is a solid methods/synthesis contribution (extending Gallardo's decade of work with five specific, defensible additions — Section 3), but descriptive maps do not clear the "changes how the field thinks about a major open question" bar that flagship-tier journals apply. Calibration point: **Overed-Sayer et al. (2025, *Nature*)**, "One-quarter of freshwater fauna threatened with extinction" — a ~90-author IUCN consortium assessment of 23,496 species — shows what "matters at that scale" looks like in this exact subfield. This project cannot and should not try to match that in scale or authorship, but it can match it in *claim type*: a generalizable, falsifiable finding about a major open question, not a snapshot map.

**The reframe, concretely — three additions on top of the existing pipeline:**

1. **Elevate the null-model test (already planned, Section 6 step 4) from a supporting check to the paper's central result.** Instead of "here is where compounding risk is concentrated" as the headline, the headline becomes "we provide the first systematic, falsifiable, global test of whether invasional meltdown is a real, detectable phenomenon — and here is the effect size and significance." Same analysis, reframed claim. No new methods cost.
2. **Retrospective/temporal validation (new — Section 6 step 5a).** Split occurrence data at a historical cutoff (candidate: 2015 or 2018, to be chosen based on data depth per species), fit the pipeline on pre-cutoff data only, then check whether the "primed but not yet hit" zones identified *from that cutoff* show significantly elevated real establishment in the post-cutoff record. This is the single highest-leverage addition: it converts a current-day snapshot into a demonstrated, verified predictive result — a materially rarer and stronger claim. **This is also the highest-risk addition**, gated on whether enough shortlisted species have sufficient clean occurrence density both before and after a defensible cutoff year (recently-arrived species especially may not) — see Section 10 and the new Section 11 action item.
3. **Anchor explicitly to Overed-Sayer et al. (2025, *Nature*)** (Section 1, "extinction-crisis anchor") for the Q4 biodiversity overlay — reframing "overlaps freshwater biodiversity hotspots" as "overlaps the specific threatened-species geography a 2025 *Nature* paper just established," which is a stronger, more citable, more reviewer-legible claim than a generic WWF ecoregions overlay alone (WWF ecoregions retained as a secondary/complementary layer, not replaced).

**What does not change:** species shortlist (Section 4), data sources (Section 5), the core SDM/stacking/null-model/co-occurrence-validation/biodiversity-overlay pipeline (Section 6 steps 1-4, 6-8), and the novelty differentiation against Gallardo's body of work (Section 3) — that differentiation argument is unaffected by and independent of this reframe.

**Honest risk assessment:** this is a reframing of ambition, not a guaranteed outcome. Flagship *Nature* itself remains unlikely (reserved for the "90-author consortium" scale of contribution); Nature Communications / Communications Biology becomes a genuine, non-frivolous stretch target if the retrospective validation succeeds (i.e., if pre-cutoff-predicted "primed" zones did in fact get invaded at above-baseline rates), with Global Change Biology / Diversity and Distributions as the credible fallback if it does not, or if data depth makes the temporal split infeasible. See revised Section 9.
